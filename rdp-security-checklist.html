RDP security checklist for a box on the public internet

A machine with remote desktop exposed to the internet is scanned within hours of coming online — not targeted, just found. The order of the fixes below matters more than the list.

First: remove it from the internet

Everything else is secondary. Remote desktop was never designed to face the open internet, and no password policy compensates for exposing it.

  • Restrict the source address. If your provider has a firewall, allow the port only from your own IP. This single change removes the overwhelming majority of the risk.
  • Or do not expose the port at all. Reach the desktop over a VPN, a bastion host, or the provider's own console. Then the port is invisible to scanners.
  • Check it is actually closed. Scan the box from outside after the change. Do not assume the rule applied — verify it.

Then: accounts

  1. No administrator account for daily use. Create a standard user for the job and for yourself; keep the admin account for administration only.
  2. Rename or disable the default administrator account if the host's image allows it. Automated attacks assume it exists under its default name.
  3. Long passphrases, unique per machine. Use a password manager; reuse is what turns one compromise into five.
  4. Enable account lockout. A handful of failed attempts should lock the account, not allow unlimited guessing.
  5. Two-factor where the host supports it. Not a substitute for restricting the port, but a strong second layer.

Operating system

  • Patch on your schedule, deliberately — the opposite of the “never restart” convenience setting. Delayed updates are an accepted risk, not a default.
  • Turn off services you do not use. Every listening service is another way in.
  • If the workload does not need a desktop at all, run it on a server OS without one. Fewer components, smaller surface — see RDP vs VPS vs dedicated.
  • Know who patches the operating system. On a self-managed VPS that is you, forever. On a managed RDP plan the provider patches the image, which removes the most common real-world failure — a box nobody has updated in eight months. If you go managed, still confirm their patch cadence and whether they reboot on their own schedule, as covered in the 24/7 setup guide; providers that manage the image themselves are the ones to compare here, for example managed RDP plans.

Monitoring — so you find out first

  • Review successful and failed logons periodically. A burst of failures from one address is routine; a success from an address you do not recognise is an incident.
  • Watch outbound traffic. Compromised boxes are usually used for something else, and that shows up as traffic you did not generate.
  • Watch for unexpected new accounts, scheduled tasks and startup entries.

If you think you have already been hit

  1. Assume the data is gone. Treat everything on the machine as disclosed.
  2. Take it offline rather than cleaning in place — you cannot trust a system you are investigating from inside.
  3. Rotate secrets that touched the box: credentials, tokens, keys, wallets, session cookies. This is the step people miss, and it is the one attackers rely on.
  4. Rebuild from a known-good image. Verification of a compromised host costs more than a rebuild, and it is less certain.
  5. Check your other hosts for the same mistake. If one box was exposed, its siblings usually were too.

Short version

  • Do not expose remote desktop to the internet; restrict the source or use a private path.
  • No admin account for daily work, lockout enabled, unique passphrases.
  • Patch deliberately and turn off what you do not use.
  • After a compromise: rebuild, and rotate every secret that touched the box.
