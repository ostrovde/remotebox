Economics

What running bots 24/7 actually costs per month

The plan price is the smallest part of the bill. What decides whether a job is worth running is the four other lines — and three of them do not appear on any invoice.

Line 1 — the machine

Straightforward: a monthly plan sized for your workload. See how much RAM a scraper needs before paying for a bigger tier. The mistake here is buying capacity for the peak minute instead of the average hour.

Line 2 — bandwidth and egress

Many hosts include a traffic allowance and bill overage. A browser-based job that loads images burns an order of magnitude more than a headless one fetching JSON from the same site. Blocking images and third-party scripts reduces this line directly and immediately.

Line 3 — proxies

If the target defends itself, the proxy bill can exceed the machine bill several times over — and it is billed per gigabyte if you use residential addresses. Headless fetching plus blocked assets is usually the single biggest saving available. The economics are covered in detail on our sister site: per GB vs per IP pricing.

Line 4 — monitoring and alerting

Small, but not zero: an uptime check, a log destination, a notification channel. Cheap to add, and it is what converts “we lost three days of data” into “we knew in twenty minutes”.

Line 5 — your time

The line nobody budgets. A job needs babysitting when:

  • Failures are silent and you only notice in the data.
  • Sessions break and need manual re-authentication.
  • The target changes its layout and parsing silently returns empty fields.
  • You size the box by trial and error instead of measuring.

Two hours a week of maintenance costs more than most hosting plans. Treat time as a line item, and count “it needs attention weekly” as a design defect rather than a fact of life.

The three cost lines almost nobody budgets

  1. Retries and wasted requests. A pool or setup that fails 30% of the time can double the real cost of every usable result — you pay for failures too.
  2. Rebuilds after incidents. An exposed box that gets compromised costs a rebuild, rotated secrets, and lost time, all of which dwarfs the hosting bill. See the RDP security checklist.
  3. Migration. Providers that cannot resize in place turn every sizing mistake into a weekend of work, which is why the ability to move between tiers matters more than a marginally cheaper plan — this is one reason we look for plans that resize without rebuilding.

How to compare two setups honestly

  • Compute cost per successful unit of work, not cost per month.
  • Include proxy traffic, retries and egress — not just the plan.
  • Add your own maintenance hours at a realistic rate.
  • Re-run the comparison monthly: targets get harder defensively over time, and pool quality drifts.

Short version

  • The plan price is the smallest cost line — time and proxies usually dominate.
  • Headless fetching with blocked assets cuts bandwidth and proxy cost at once.
  • Budget for retries, incident rebuilds and migration, not just hosting.
  • Compare cost per successful unit of work, and re-check it monthly.
